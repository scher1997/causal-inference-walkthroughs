TipWalkthrough summary

Treatment is assigned by a hard cutoff on a running variable. Units just on either side are nearly identical, so the jump in the outcome at the cutoff estimates the effect for units at the threshold. The assumption is that potential outcomes are smooth at the cutoff, so nothing else jumps there and people cannot precisely sort across it.

  • Use local linear regression within a bandwidth, with a triangular kernel. The bandwidth trades bias against variance, so check that results are stable across a range.
  • Avoid global high-order polynomials. They give noisy, misleading estimates at the boundary.
  • Run validity checks. A density test catches manipulation, such as bunching just above a quota. Pre-treatment covariates should show no jump, and placebo cutoffs should show no effect.
  • The estimate is local to the cutoff, so extrapolating far from it needs extra assumptions.

Setting: treatment is assigned by a strict rule on a continuous running variable: customers with a risk score ≥ 0 get a premium offer, students above a test cutoff get a scholarship, stores above a revenue threshold get a new process.

Units just above and just below the cutoff are nearly identical, so the jump in the outcome at the cutoff is the causal effect for units at the threshold (Equation 4.1):

\[\tau_{RD} = \lim_{x \downarrow c} E[Y \mid X=x] - \lim_{x \uparrow c} E[Y \mid X=x] \tag{4.1}\]

ImportantIdentifying assumption: smoothness at the cutoff

potential outcomes are smooth (continuous) in the running variable at the cutoff — nothing else jumps there, and units can’t precisely sort themselves across it.

  1. Plot binned means — the RD plot.
  2. Local linear regression with a kernel within a bandwidth.
  3. Sensitivity to bandwidth, kernel and polynomial order.
  4. Validity checks: density (manipulation) test, covariate continuity, placebo cutoffs.

a jump of 0.5 at a cutoff of 0, with a nonlinear underlying relationship whose curvature differs on each side of the cutoff.

    coef = np.polyfit(sub.score, sub.y, 1)
    xs = np.linspace(sub.score.min(), sub.score.max(), 50)

ax.axvline(CUTOFF, ls="--", c="grey")
ax.set(title="RD plot: binned means + local linear fits (h = 0.3)", xlabel="running variable (score)", ylabel="outcome")
plt.show()

4.1 Local linear estimate

Fit separate linear regressions on each side within bandwidth \(h\), weighting observations closer to the cutoff more (triangular kernel1). The coefficient on the treatment dummy is the jump. Local linear (not local constant) is standard because it has much smaller bias at a boundary.

1 Triangular kernel: an observation’s weight falls linearly from 1 at the cutoff to 0 at the edge of the bandwidth.

Code 
r = rdd.local_poly_rd(df.score, df.y, cutoff=CUTOFF, bandwidth=0.3, kernel="triangular", order=1)
print(f"RD estimate {r.estimate:.3f}  (SE {r.se:.3f}, 95% CI {r.ci[0]:.3f} to {r.ci[1]:.3f})  "
      f"n_left={r.n_left}, n_right={r.n_right}; truth = {TRUE_EFFECT}")

4.2 Bandwidth sensitivity

The bandwidth is a bias–variance trade-off: narrow = less bias from curvature but noisier; wide = more precise but leans on the functional form. Results should be stable over a reasonable range. (In production, use the MSE-optimal bandwidth and robust bias-corrected CIs from rdrobust.)

Code 
hs = np.round(np.arange(0.05, 1.01, 0.05), 2)
sens = pd.DataFrame([vars(rdd.local_poly_rd(df.score, df.y, CUTOFF, bandwidth=h)) for h in hs])

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.errorbar(sens.bandwidth, sens.estimate, yerr=1.96 * sens.se, fmt="o", capsize=3)
ax.axhline(TRUE_EFFECT, c="green", ls=":", label="truth")
ax.set(title="Local linear estimate vs bandwidth", xlabel="bandwidth h", ylabel="RD estimate (95% CI)")
ax.legend(); plt.show()

In Figure 4.2, wide bandwidths drift away from the truth: a straight line can’t follow the cubic curvature over a wide range. Local quadratic is more robust to wide windows at the cost of variance:

4.3 Validity checks

(a) Density / manipulation test. If units can game the score (e.g. salespeople nudging deals just over a quota), there will be bunching just above the cutoff, and those units differ from the ones just below. We compare a clean dataset to one where 60% of units just below the cutoff pushed themselves over.

Code 
manip = simulate.rdd_data(n=5000, cutoff=CUTOFF, effect=TRUE_EFFECT, manipulation=0.6, seed=0)
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 3.8), sharey=True)
for ax, (name, d) in zip(axes, {"clean": df, "manipulated": manip}.items()):
    t = rdd.density_test(d.score, CUTOFF, bandwidth=0.3, bin_width=0.02)
    ax.hist(d.score, bins=np.arange(-1, 1.001, 0.04), color="C0", alpha=0.7)
    ax.axvline(CUTOFF, ls="--", c="k")
    ax.set(title=f"{name}: log density ratio = {t['log_ratio']:.2f}, p = {t['p']:.3g}", xlabel="score")
plt.show()

(b) Covariate continuity. Pre-treatment covariates can’t be affected by treatment, so they should show no jump. Run the same RD with the covariate as the outcome.

(c) Placebo cutoffs. At fake cutoffs (using only data on one side of the true one, so the real jump doesn’t leak in) there should be no effect.

4.4 Practical notes

  • Don’t use global high-order polynomials (e.g. a 5th-order fit over the whole range). They give noisy, misleading estimates at the boundary (Gelman and Imbens 2019). Use local linear/quadratic.
  • Fuzzy RD: if crossing the cutoff only raises the probability of treatment, the jump in the outcome divided by the jump in treatment take-up is a local IV/LATE estimate (rdrobust handles this with fuzzy=).
  • External validity: the estimate is local to units near the cutoff. Extrapolating to units far away requires extra assumptions.
  • Discrete running variables (e.g. integer scores) need care — few mass points near the cutoff; see rdrobust/rdlocrand guidance.
  • Libraries: rdrobust (optimal bandwidths, robust bias-corrected inference), rddensity (Cattaneo–Jansson–Ma density test), rdlocrand (local randomization inference).

TipWhy use local linear regression instead of a high-order global polynomial?

Global high-order polynomials give noisy, misleading estimates at the boundary (Gelman and Imbens 2019). Local linear fits have much smaller bias at a boundary and use only nearby data.

Gelman, Andrew, and Guido Imbens. 2019. “Why High-Order Polynomials Should Not Be Used in Regression Discontinuity Designs.” Journal of Business & Economic Statistics.
TipWhat does the density test detect, and what does a significant result mean?

It detects bunching just above or below the cutoff, a sign that units sorted themselves across it. Then units on either side are no longer comparable and the smoothness assumption fails.

## Local linear estimate

Fit separate linear regressions on each side within bandwidth $h$, weighting observations closer to the cutoff more (triangular kernel[^kernel]). The coefficient on the treatment dummy is the jump. Local *linear* (not local constant) is standard because it has much smaller bias at a boundary.

```{python}
r = rdd.local_poly_rd(df.score, df.y, cutoff=CUTOFF, bandwidth=0.3, kernel="triangular", order=1)  # <1>
print(f"RD estimate {r.estimate:.3f}  (SE {r.se:.3f}, 95% CI {r.ci[0]:.3f} to {r.ci[1]:.3f})  "
      f"n_left={r.n_left}, n_right={r.n_right}; truth = {TRUE_EFFECT}")
```

1. A kernel-weighted local linear fit on each side of the cutoff: triangular weights fall to zero at distance `bandwidth` (0.3) and `order=1` is a straight line. The jump at the cutoff is the estimate, with HC1 standard errors.

## Practical notes

- **Don't use global high-order polynomials** (e.g. a 5th-order fit over the whole range). They give noisy, misleading estimates at the boundary [@gelman2019]. Use local linear/quadratic.
- **Fuzzy RD:** if crossing the cutoff only *raises the probability* of treatment, the jump in the outcome divided by the jump in treatment take-up is a local IV/LATE estimate (`rdrobust` handles this with `fuzzy=`).
- **External validity:** the estimate is local to units near the cutoff. Extrapolating to units far away requires extra assumptions.
- **Discrete running variables** (e.g. integer scores) need care — few mass points near the cutoff; see `rdrobust`/`rdlocrand` guidance.
- **Libraries:** `rdrobust` (optimal bandwidths, robust bias-corrected inference), `rddensity` (Cattaneo–Jansson–Ma density test), `rdlocrand` (local randomization inference).

[^kernel]: Triangular kernel: an observation's weight falls linearly from 1 at the cutoff to 0 at the edge of the bandwidth.
